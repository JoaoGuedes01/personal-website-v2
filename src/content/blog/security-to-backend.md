I didn't start out building products. I started out trying to break them, or at least trying to
understand how they'd break. When I moved into backend engineering, I expected to leave that
behind. Instead it quietly rewired how I write code.

## Threat modelling is just design with the lights on

Security people ask a specific question about every system: *what happens when this is used by
someone who doesn't wish it well?* Turns out that's a fantastic design question even when no one's
attacking you. Bad input, partial failures, race conditions — they're all just "hostile" states
your code will eventually meet by accident.

## Blast radius over cleverness

The security habit I value most is thinking about blast radius. Before shipping, I ask how far a
mistake can travel. A clever solution with a huge blast radius loses to a boring one that fails
small, every time.

## Trust boundaries make good architecture

Knowing exactly where data crosses from "I control this" to "I don't" tells you where to validate,
where to log, and where to be paranoid. That single line — *where's the boundary?* — has untangled
more design arguments for me than any pattern.

Different job, same instincts. I just point them at building now instead of breaking.
